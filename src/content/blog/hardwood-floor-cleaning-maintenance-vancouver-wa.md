Most of the hardwood damage we are asked to fix in Clark County homes was not caused by the family walking on the floor. It was caused by how the floor was cleaned.

That is an easy thing to get wrong, because plenty of products that are sold for floors are bad for wood floors specifically, and the damage is slow enough that nobody connects it to the cause.

## The one rule that matters most

**Water is the enemy, and steam is water at its most aggressive.**

A hardwood floor is finished on top, but the seams between boards are not sealed. Standing water finds those seams, and steam actively drives moisture into them under pressure. The board edges take on moisture, swell, and the floor begins to cup — the edges rising higher than the middle.

Steam mops are superb on tile. On hardwood they are a slow-motion way of destroying a floor, and by the time the cupping is visible the moisture has been getting in for months.

Damp, not wet. Always.

## What to use

A cleaner formulated for hardwood, sprayed onto the mop head rather than poured onto the floor, with the mop wrung out properly.

That is genuinely the whole method. The reason to buy a wood-specific product rather than improvise is that the common household improvisations all cause a specific problem:

- **Vinegar** is acidic and dulls polyurethane over time. It is a fine cleaner and a poor choice for a finished wood floor.
- **Dish soap** leaves a film. It looks clean, then looks hazy, and the haze builds.
- **Oil soaps** leave a residue that is genuinely difficult to remove and that interferes with adhesion if the floor is ever recoated. We have had to strip floors before we could recoat them because of this.
- **All-purpose sprays** vary enormously and many are alkaline enough to damage the finish.

## The part that actually protects the floor

Grit.

Sand, dust and the fine grit tracked in on shoes is abrasive, and every step grinds it into the finish. That is what wears a floor out, far more than the walking itself.

Which means the highest-value maintenance is the least glamorous: sweep or vacuum often. A vacuum with the beater bar switched off, or a hard-floor setting, is ideal. In the Pacific Northwest, where wet shoes bring in more than dry ones do, a decent doormat at every entrance does more for a hardwood floor than any cleaning product.

Felt pads under furniture legs are the other one. They cost almost nothing and they prevent the dragging scratches that are otherwise inevitable.

## Humidity, and why it matters here

Our climate swings — wet winters, drier summers, and heated indoor air that is drier still. Wood responds to that by expanding and contracting slightly through the year.

That is normal and not something to fight. What is worth doing is avoiding the extremes: keeping indoor humidity reasonably steady reduces how far the boards travel, which reduces seasonal gapping in winter. If you see thin gaps open between boards in the cold months and close again by summer, that is the floor behaving as it should, not a defect.

## Dealing with what gets spilled

Speed matters far more than technique. A spill wiped up within a few minutes does nothing to a properly finished floor. The same spill left overnight, particularly against a seam, is a different conversation.

Pet accidents deserve specific mention because urine is genuinely damaging — it penetrates the finish and stains the wood underneath, and sanding often cannot reach the discolouration once it has soaked in. Found quickly, no harm. Found behind a piece of furniture a week later, that is usually board replacement.

## When cleaning is no longer the answer

If the floor is clean and still looks dull, cleaning harder will not help. What you are seeing is a worn finish, which is a different problem with a much better solution.

A screen and recoat abrades the old finish and lays down a fresh protective coat without sanding into the wood. It restores the look, it is far cheaper and faster than a full refinish, and it resets the clock on the layer that protects your floor. Our [hardwood refinishing guide](/blog/hardwood-floor-refinishing-vancouver-guide/) explains when a recoat is enough and when a full sanding is needed.

If the marks catch your fingernail, they are into the wood and a recoat will not remove them. If they do not, a recoat probably will.
