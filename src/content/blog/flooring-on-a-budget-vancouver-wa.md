There is a version of saving money on flooring that works and a version that costs you more within a few years. The difference is knowing which parts of a quote are the floor and which parts are the labour around it.

Here is where we would cut, and where we would not.

## Where to save

**Do your own tear-out.** Pulling up carpet is unpleasant and not technical. If you are willing to do it, that is a genuine reduction with no downside. Tell us before we quote and we will price around it.

We would not extend that to glued vinyl or tile set in mortar — those need specific tools, and repairing a subfloor damaged during amateur removal costs more than the removal would have.

**Move your own furniture.** Same logic. If you can clear the rooms yourself, that is time you are not paying for.

**Choose mid-range, not top-of-range.** The jump from cheapest to mid-range buys you a meaningfully better wear layer and a floor that lasts. The jump from mid-range to premium mostly buys you appearance — a more convincing texture, a more interesting grain. If budget is tight, that second jump is the one to skip. The first one is not.

**Do it all at once.** Every separate visit carries its own setup, and doing three rooms together costs less than three rooms separately. There is also a matching problem: dye lots shift, and product lines get discontinued, so a floor bought in stages may not match itself.

**Ask about timing.** Product availability and supplier promotions move through the year. If your project is not urgent, tell us — sometimes waiting a few weeks lands you a better price on the same material.

## Where not to save

**Subfloor preparation.** This is the one we will argue about. It is invisible on handover day, it is the easiest line to cut from a quote, and it is the reason floors fail early.

An uneven subfloor under a click-together floor causes flexing at the joints until the locking mechanism fails. Under tile it cracks grout. Moisture in a slab under the wrong adhesive breaks the bond. Every one of those is a full replacement two years later, which is not a saving.

If a quote comes in noticeably cheaper than the others, ask what it assumes about the subfloor. That is usually where the difference is.

**Underlayment.** It is a small line item that determines how the floor sounds and feels underfoot. Skipping it is how a floor ends up clicky and hollow, and there is no fixing it afterwards without lifting the whole thing.

**Wear layer on a busy floor.** In a hallway, a kitchen or anywhere with a dog, the wear layer is what you are actually buying. Saving on it in a low-traffic bedroom is fine. Saving on it in the main route through the house means replacing the floor early.

## The false economy that catches people most

Choosing a floor for a room based on price rather than on what the room does.

Laminate in a bathroom because it was cheap. Standard carpet in an entryway because it was on offer. Peel-and-stick in a kitchen. Each of these is a saving on day one and a replacement within a few years, because the material was never suited to what the room puts it through.

Match the material to the room first, then find the best price within the materials that actually work. Not the other way round.

## What we would actually tell you in your kitchen

If the budget is genuinely fixed, our advice is usually to reduce the scope rather than the specification — do fewer rooms properly rather than every room cheaply. A well-specified floor in the two rooms you use most will serve you better than a compromised one throughout.
