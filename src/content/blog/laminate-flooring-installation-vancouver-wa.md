Laminate has a reputation as the floor anyone can install. The planks click together, there is no glue and no nails, and a square bedroom really can be done in a weekend. That reputation is also why so many laminate floors in Clark County fail early: joints that open up, edges that swell, boards that bounce and click when you walk across them.

Almost none of those failures come from the product. They come from what happened, or did not happen, before the first plank went down. This guide covers what a proper laminate flooring installation in a Vancouver, WA home involves, so you know what you are paying for and what to watch for. If you are still deciding whether laminate is the right floor, start with our [laminate buyer's guide](/blog/laminate-flooring-vancouver-wa/).

## Laminate is a floating floor, and that sets the rules

Laminate planks lock to each other along the edges and ends. They are not fastened to the subfloor at all. The whole floor sits on an underlayment as a single sheet, held in place by its own weight and the trim around the room.

Two things follow from that, and every rule below traces back to them.

1. **The floor moves.** The core of a laminate plank is high-density wood fiber. It expands and contracts with temperature and humidity, and the whole floor moves as one unit. It needs room at every edge.
2. **The floor bridges nothing.** A floating floor follows whatever is under it. A dip in the subfloor becomes a spot where the planks flex every time someone steps there, and a flexing joint eventually breaks.

## What has to be true about your subfloor

**Flat.** Most laminate manufacturers allow no more than about 3/16 of an inch of variation over ten feet. High spots get sanded or ground down. Low spots get filled with floor patch or self-leveling compound. This is the step that gets skipped on rushed jobs, and it is the most common reason laminate fails. A thicker plank or a thicker pad does not fix an uneven subfloor. It hides it for a year.

**Sound.** Loose subfloor panels get screwed down, squeaks get chased while the floor is open, and any soft or water-damaged sections are replaced before they are covered.

**Dry.** This is the one that matters most in the Pacific Northwest. Laminate's surface is tough, but its core is wood fiber, and moisture coming up from below is more dangerous to it than a spill on top. Many Vancouver homes sit over crawlspaces, and plenty of daylight basements and slab-on-grade additions have concrete floors that release moisture for years. We check the subfloor's moisture before installation, and over concrete a vapor barrier always goes down first.

## Acclimation: two days that save the floor

Laminate needs to sit in the room where it will be installed, usually for about 48 hours, with the house at the temperature and humidity you actually live at. The boxes stay flat and closed, stacked off the concrete.

In Vancouver this matters most from November through March. Planks stored in a cold garage and installed the same morning will grow once the furnace warms them. If the floor was installed tight, it has nowhere to go, and it peaks at the seams. The reverse happens to a floor installed in a warm, humid August and then dried out by winter heating: it shrinks and the end joints gap. Letting the material settle first is free.

## Underlayment: one layer, the right one

Underlayment does three jobs under laminate: it smooths over tiny subfloor imperfections, it quiets the hollow sound floating floors are known for, and over concrete it blocks moisture.

- **If the plank has an attached pad,** do not add another cushion under it. Doubling the pad makes the floor too soft, the joints flex, and the locking edges break. This is one of the most common DIY mistakes we see.
- **Over a concrete slab,** a vapor barrier is required even when the plank has an attached pad. The pad is for comfort and sound. It is not a moisture barrier.
- **On an upper floor,** a denser acoustic underlayment makes a real difference to the room below. Condos and townhomes often have an HOA sound rating the underlayment has to meet, so check before you buy.

## Installing over existing floors

Floating over what is already there saves tear-out cost and a day of dust.

**Laminate can go over:** sheet vinyl that is fully adhered, ceramic or porcelain tile that is flat and solid (deep grout lines may need filling), and hardwood that is solidly fastened over a wood subfloor.

**Laminate cannot go over:** carpet or pad, another floating floor, cushioned or perimeter-glued vinyl, or anything loose, uneven or damp.

Every added layer raises the floor. That affects door swing, the transition into the next room, and appliance clearance. A dishwasher can end up trapped under the countertop by a new floor installed in front of it. We measure these at the estimate, before anything is ordered.

## Expansion gaps, transitions and door jambs

- **A perimeter gap** at every wall, cabinet, pipe and door frame, typically between a quarter and a half inch depending on the manufacturer. Baseboard or quarter round covers it. Trim is nailed to the wall, never down through the floor, because pinning a floating floor in one spot is as bad as leaving no gap.
- **Transition strips** at doorways and where laminate meets another floor. Long runs need them too. Many products call for a T-molding once a floor runs past a certain length, often around 40 feet, and an open floor plan can reach that quickly. Where the transition lands is worth deciding on purpose rather than discovering on installation day.
- **Undercut door jambs.** The casing is trimmed so the plank slides underneath it. A plank notched around a door frame with a bead of caulk is the clearest sign of a rushed install.
- **Staggered end joints,** with no short slivers at the ends of rows. Layout is planned from the most visible wall so cuts fall where they will not be noticed.

## Kitchens, bathrooms and other wet areas

Water-resistant laminate has widened where laminate makes sense, and it does well in kitchens, entries and powder rooms. Installation details change in these rooms.

**Cabinets do not sit on a floating floor.** A run of base cabinets or an island pins the floor in place and stops it from moving, which causes buckling somewhere else. Laminate is installed up to the cabinets, with the gap covered by toe kick or trim.

**Water warranties usually depend on the install.** Many water-resistant laminate lines require the perimeter gap in wet areas to be sealed with flexible silicone so water cannot run under the edge of the floor. If that step is skipped, the water warranty usually goes with it. Ask what the specific product requires.

**Full bathrooms are still a stretch.** A room with a tub or shower in daily use sees standing water and steam. We will usually point you toward [luxury vinyl plank](/luxury-vinyl-plank/) or tile there, and our comparison of [LVP vs. laminate in a rainy climate](/blog/lvp-vs-laminate-rainy-climate/) explains why.

## Laminate on stairs

Stairs are the exception to the floating rule. Laminate on a staircase is glued and fastened, tread by tread, with a matching stair nosing on each step. It is slow, precise work, and it is priced per step rather than per square foot. If your project includes a staircase, make sure it appears as its own line on every quote you compare.

## What installation day looks like

1. Furniture moved. Our crews move standard household furniture. You handle fragile items, electronics and valuables.
2. Old flooring removed and hauled away, if it is coming out.
3. Subfloor checked, cleaned and prepped: fastening, sanding or grinding, patching, moisture check.
4. Vapor barrier and underlayment laid as the subfloor and product require.
5. Layout planned: starting wall, plank direction, stagger, where transitions and cuts will fall.
6. Planks installed row by row, with jambs undercut and gaps held at every fixed object.
7. Baseboard or quarter round, transitions and stair nosings.
8. Cleanup and a walkthrough with you.

A single room is usually one day. A full main level runs two to four days depending on size and prep. There is no adhesive to cure, so furniture can go back as soon as the trim is on. Our [flooring installation page](/flooring-installation/) lists what is included as standard.

## What drives the cost of laminate installation

Installed laminate in the Vancouver area typically lands around $4 to $9 per square foot, which is why it shows up so often in our guides to [flooring on a budget](/blog/flooring-on-a-budget-vancouver-wa/) and [flooring for rental properties](/blog/flooring-for-rental-properties-vancouver-wa/). Where you land in that range depends on:

- The product: thickness, wear rating and water-resistance
- Square footage, plus 5 to 10 percent for cuts and waste
- Tear-out and disposal of the existing floor
- Subfloor prep, which ranges from nothing to a full day of leveling
- Underlayment and vapor barrier
- Stairs, priced per step
- Transitions, baseboard and trim

## When DIY makes sense, and when it does not

Laminate is the most DIY-friendly floor there is, and for the right room installing it yourself is a sensible way to save money. A square bedroom, a flat plywood subfloor, no stairs and no concrete is a realistic weekend project with a miter saw and patience.

Professional installation earns its cost when the job includes any of these: a concrete slab, a subfloor that is visibly out of flat, a kitchen with an island, a staircase, several rooms that need to flow together without awkward transitions, or a water-resistant product whose warranty depends on how the edges are sealed. It also gives you one company accountable for both the material and the work.
