A basement in Clark County or Portland is the one room where the floor you want and the floor that will last can be very different things. Upstairs, the choice is mostly about looks and wear. Downstairs, it starts with the concrete, and in one of the wettest metro areas in the country, that concrete is rarely as dry as it looks.

This guide covers how we read a basement before recommending anything, and where each type of flooring fits below grade.

## Start with the slab, not the sample

A basement slab sits in the ground. Moisture moves up through concrete from the soil underneath, slowly and constantly, and through a wet Northwest winter there is a lot of it to move. A floor that traps that moisture, or that takes it up, fails from underneath.

Before we talk about products, we look for the signs a slab is giving off moisture:

- White, chalky mineral deposits on the concrete or along the base of the walls.
- Water lines or staining on the walls or on old carpet backing.
- A musty smell that does not clear when the room is aired out.
- Rust on the feet of anything metal that sits on the floor.

Then the slab gets tested. What we find decides which floors make sense. A dry, well-sealed basement can take almost anything except solid hardwood. A basement that has taken on water before needs a floor that can get wet and come back.

## Luxury vinyl plank: the default for a reason

For most basements here, [luxury vinyl plank](/luxury-vinyl-plank/) is where we start. The plank itself is waterproof. It floats over the slab on a vapor barrier or an attached underlayment, so it does not depend on bonding to concrete that might be damp. It is warmer and softer underfoot than tile, and a good wood or stone visual makes the basement feel like part of the house.

If water ever does get in, a floating floor can be lifted at the edges so the slab dries, and the planks themselves survive. That is not true of laminate, carpet or wood.

## Porcelain tile: the most water-tolerant floor

[Porcelain tile](/tile-flooring/) is the floor that cares least about water. It works well in a basement bathroom, a laundry area, or a basement with a history of seepage. The tradeoff is comfort: tile on a below-grade slab is cold and hard. Area rugs help in living spaces, and in a bathroom or laundry that matters less.

## Carpet: for the dry basement

Carpet earns its place in a basement media room, a playroom or a guest bedroom. It takes the chill off the concrete and soaks up sound, which is why a basement theater on a hard floor sounds harsh. Berber and loop carpets are a common basement choice because they hold up to traffic.

The condition is that the basement has to stay dry. Carpet and pad hold moisture against the slab, and a wet pad is where the musty basement smell comes from. If you have ever had water down there, pick a hard floor and add rugs you can lift out and dry. Our [carpet](/carpet-flooring/) page covers the styles.

## Engineered hardwood: possible, with conditions

Solid hardwood is the wrong product below grade. It takes up moisture from the slab and cups.

Engineered hardwood can work. It has a real wood surface over a stable core, and it can be glued down or floated over a moisture barrier once the slab has been tested. In a dry, finished basement where you want real wood to match the floors upstairs, it is a reasonable choice. In a basement with any water history, we will tell you plainly that vinyl plank or tile is the better floor. Our guide to [engineered vs. solid hardwood](/blog/engineered-vs-solid-hardwood-vancouver/) explains the difference in construction.

## Laminate: think twice

Laminate is tough on top and vulnerable at the core. Many laminates have a fiberboard core that swells if water reaches it and does not shrink back. Some newer laminates are sold as water resistant and handle spills well, but a basement with any seepage risk is a poor place to test that. If you like the look of laminate, [waterproof laminate vs. LVP](/blog/waterproof-laminate-vs-lvp-vancouver-wa/) is worth reading before you decide.

## The other basement details

**Flatness.** Basement slabs often have dips, humps and cracks. Floating floors need a flat surface or the joints flex and fail. Expect some grinding or patching before the floor goes down.

**Height.** Basement doors and stair landings sometimes leave little room for a thick floor. We measure where the new floor meets the bottom stair, so the last step does not end up shorter than the rest.

**Humidity.** A dehumidifier running through the wet season does more for a basement floor than any product upgrade. It keeps the air dry, which keeps the floor and everything on it happier.

**Sound and warmth.** An underlayment under a floating floor adds a little cushion and cuts the hollow sound some hard floors make over concrete.

Older Portland homes often have full basements with original concrete and decades of patching. Newer homes in Clark County are more likely to be on a crawlspace or slab, with a daylight basement where a lot slopes. Both get the same first step: look at the concrete, test it, then choose.
