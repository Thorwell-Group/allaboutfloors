New floors are one of the most visible changes you can make to a house, and one of the easiest to get wrong in the planning. Most problems we see aren't installation problems. They come from decisions made in the wrong order. Baseboards go back on before the floor height is known, cabinets get set where a floating floor needs to run, or a budget leaves nothing for what's under the old carpet.

This guide is for Clark County homeowners planning a flooring remodel, whether it's a single floor through the main level or new floors as part of a kitchen or whole-house renovation. It covers what to decide first, where flooring fits in the order of work, the permit and old-house rules that apply here, and how to set a realistic budget and timeline.

## Step 1: Settle the scope before you shop

The biggest decision isn't the material. It's which rooms, and whether they connect.

- **Continuous or room by room.** One floor running through the entry, living room, dining room, kitchen and hallway makes a house feel bigger and removes transition strips. Doing it in stages is fine, but buy all the material at once so the dye lot matches, or choose a floor that will still be in production when you come back to it.
- **Wet rooms.** Kitchens, bathrooms, laundry rooms and entries need a waterproof floor or tile. That often decides the product for the whole connected area. See our [waterproof flooring guide](/blog/waterproof-flooring-vancouver-wa/).
- **Stairs.** If the stairs sit between two rooms getting hardwood or LVP, decide now whether they're changing too. Leaving carpeted stairs between new hard floors is the most common thing people wish they'd done differently. Our [hardwood stairs guide](/blog/hardwood-stairs-vancouver-wa/) covers the options.
- **Floor height.** New flooring, plus underlayment or backer board, changes the height at doors, thresholds, the dishwasher opening and the top and bottom stair. Plan it now, not on install day.

If you're still deciding between materials, our [Pacific Northwest flooring guide](/blog/best-flooring-pacific-northwest-climate/) compares them for this climate, and [LVP vs. laminate](/blog/lvp-vs-laminate-rainy-climate/) covers the most common decision.

## Step 2: Get a real consultation, not a phone number

An accurate quote needs someone in the house. A good flooring consultation includes:

1. **Measuring every room,** including closets, and calculating waste for the layout and material
2. **Checking the subfloor** for flatness, squeaks, soft spots and old water damage
3. **Reading moisture** on any concrete slab and looking at the crawlspace if there is one
4. **Finding out what's under the current floor,** including how many layers and whether any old vinyl may need testing
5. **Noting stairs, transitions, door clearances, appliances and trim**
6. **Looking at samples in your own light,** at the times of day you actually use the rooms

**What to have ready:** the rooms you're considering, a rough budget range, your timeline, whether pets or kids will be home, and photos of anything you like. Knowing whether cabinets, paint or countertops are changing too lets the quote reflect the real order of work.

You should leave with a written, itemized quote that lists materials, tear-out, prep, leveling, transitions, stairs and trim separately. That's the only way to compare one company with another. ## Step 3: Put flooring in the right place in the order of work

In a bigger remodel, flooring is usually one of the last major trades, but not the very last. A typical sequence:

1. **Demolition** and removal of old flooring that's coming out
2. **Structural, plumbing and electrical** work, including moving a sink, adding outlets in an island, or fixing framing
3. **Subfloor repairs and leveling**
4. **Drywall and texture**
5. **Prime and paint walls and ceilings,** before the new floor goes in
6. **Cabinets,** if the floor is floating. Tile can go before or after cabinets.
7. **Flooring**
8. **Baseboards and trim,** then caulk and final trim paint
9. **Countertops,** appliances and fixtures, with the new floor protected
10. **Touch-up paint and final cleaning**

Three rules matter most:

- **Floating floors stop at the cabinets.** LVP and laminate need room to expand. Planks pinned under a cabinet run or a heavy island will buckle.
- **Paint first, baseboards after.** Pulling baseboards to install the floor and reinstalling them after gives a cleaner result than adding shoe molding to cover a gap.
- **Site-finished hardwood goes last among the messy trades.** Sanding dust and finish fumes don't mix with other trades working in the house.

## Step 4: Know the rules that apply in Clark County

**Building permits.** Replacing floor coverings is finish work, and Clark County's building code lists floor coverings, cabinets, countertops and moldings among the work that doesn't require a building permit. That changes if the remodel includes structural repairs to joists or framing, or plumbing or electrical work, which can need their own permits. Vancouver, Camas, Battle Ground, Ridgefield and the other cities run their own permit offices, so check with the one that covers your address.

**Asbestos in older homes.** Vinyl floor tile, sheet vinyl backing and black mastic adhesive often contained asbestos in homes built through the 1980s. In Clark County, the Southwest Clean Air Agency requires an asbestos survey before renovation work that disturbs building materials. For owner-occupied single-family homes where the owner does the work, the agency's guidance lets homeowners survey their own homes, but suspect material still has to be handled under its rules. When a contractor does the work, the survey needs to be done by a certified inspector. Often the simplest answer is not to disturb old flooring at all: floating LVP or laminate can go over sound, flat, well-bonded old vinyl. Check SWCAA's current requirements for your project.

**Lead paint.** In homes built before 1978, baseboards, door casings and thresholds may have lead paint, and flooring work disturbs exactly that trim. Contractors doing paid renovation work in pre-1978 homes are subject to the federal Renovation, Repair and Painting rule, which Washington administers through the Department of Commerce. Ask any contractor how they handle it.

## Step 5: Build a budget with room for what's underneath

A flooring budget has four parts:

- **Material,** including 5 to 10 percent extra for cuts and waste, and more for tile, diagonal layouts and herringbone
- **Installation labor**
- **Prep:** tear-out and disposal, subfloor repair, leveling, moisture barriers and backer board
- **Finish details:** transitions, stair treads and nosing, baseboards and shoe molding, appliance pull and reset, moving furniture

Then add a **10 to 15 percent contingency.** Nearly every surprise in a flooring remodel is under the old floor: a second layer of vinyl, a soft spot by the toilet or dishwasher, a subfloor that's out of flat, or old tile that needs testing.

Where to spend and where to save: spend on the floor in the rooms that take the most water and traffic, and on prep, because no floor outlasts a bad subfloor. Save on bedrooms and closets, where a less expensive line in the same color family can work. ## Step 6: A realistic timeline

| Stage | Typical time |
| --- | --- |
| Consultation, samples and decision | 1 to 2 weeks |
| Final measure and order | A few days for stocked products; several weeks for special orders |
| Acclimation in the house (hardwood, many LVP and laminate lines) | Per manufacturer, often 2 to 3 days |
| Tear-out and subfloor prep | Same day as install, or 1 extra day |
| Installation, typical main floor | 3 to 5 working days |
| Tile and grout cure, site-finished hardwood cure | Several extra days before full use |
| Baseboards, touch-up and walkthrough | 1 day |

From first consultation to finished floors, two to six weeks is typical for a flooring-only remodel. If other trades are involved, the flooring slot is usually the date to protect, so book it once cabinets and paint have firm dates. ## Living in the house while it happens

Most Clark County families stay home through a flooring remodel. A few things make it easier:

- **Phase the work** so one part of the house stays usable. Bedrooms one day, main living area the next.
- **Clear small items** like décor, closet floors and everything under the beds. Ask in advance who moves the furniture and what it costs.
- **Plan for the kitchen** to be out of service while the appliances are pulled.
- **Pets** need somewhere else to be on install days, especially with adhesives, finishes and open doors.
- **Keep the heat on** at normal living temperature. Flooring is acclimated to the house you live in, not a cold one.
