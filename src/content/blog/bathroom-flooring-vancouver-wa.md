Bathrooms are the smallest rooms we floor and the ones that go wrong most often. The reason is simple: everywhere else in the house, water is an accident. In a bathroom, water is the point.

Here's how to choose a bathroom floor in a Vancouver home, and the one thing worth checking before you choose anything.

## Check the subfloor first

Before you pick a color, press your foot down around the base of the toilet and along the front of the tub. If anything feels soft, spongy, or gives slightly, stop shopping.

A wax ring that fails doesn't announce itself. It weeps a small amount of water into the subfloor on every flush, and it can do that for years. By the time the floor feels soft, the plywood underneath has gone punky and sometimes the joists are involved. Putting a beautiful new floor over a rotten subfloor buys you a couple of years and then the same failure.

This is the single most common surprise we find in Vancouver bathrooms, particularly in homes built before the mid-90s. It's fixable, we cut out the compromised section and replace it, but it needs to be part of the estimate rather than a mid-job phone call.

## Porcelain tile: the standard for a reason

For a primary bathroom, or any bathroom with a shower, [tile](/tile-flooring/) is the material we recommend most.

It's genuinely waterproof, not water-resistant. It doesn't care about steam. It handles a tub overflow without drama. And with modern large-format tile, you can get the look with far less grout to maintain than the 12x12 squares that came out of the 90s.

Two things to plan for. First, tile is cold, which is a real consideration in a climate where "cold tile" describes most of the year. Second, it's hard, dropped items break and long stretches of standing are less comfortable. Both of these are solvable: electric radiant heat under the tile fixes the temperature problem completely, and a bath mat fixes the rest.

## Waterproof LVP: warmer, cheaper, and genuinely fine

[Luxury vinyl plank](/luxury-vinyl-plank/) has changed what's possible in secondary bathrooms. The core doesn't absorb water, so a splashy kid's bath or an overflowing sink is a wipe-up rather than a replacement.

It's noticeably warmer underfoot than tile, it costs less, and it installs faster. For a powder room, a guest bath, or a kids' bathroom, it's often the smarter allocation of budget, and it lets you spend more on the primary bath.

The caveat is the same one that applies in any room: the plank is waterproof, the installation isn't hermetic. For a bathroom, that means caulking the perimeter properly and paying attention to the tub and toilet penetrations. Done right, it holds up. Done casually, water finds the edges.

## What we'd steer you away from

**Laminate.** The core is wood fiber. A bathroom is the one room where that's disqualifying. Our [LVP vs. laminate comparison](/blog/lvp-vs-laminate-rainy-climate/) covers why the difference matters most in wet rooms.

**Peel-and-stick vinyl.** It's tempting for a small powder room, and it goes down in an afternoon. In a bathroom, the adhesive gives up at the seams once humidity gets under it, usually within a couple of years. We wrote about [peel-and-stick versus professional vinyl](/blog/peel-and-stick-vs-professional-vinyl-vancouver-wa/) if you're weighing it.

**Solid hardwood.** Beautiful, wrong room. The humidity cycle alone will cup it.

## Small room, small budget, real leverage

A typical Vancouver bathroom is 35 to 60 square feet. That's the smallest material bill in the house, which means it's the one room where upgrading to the material you actually want costs very little in absolute dollars.

It's also the room where labor is the larger share of the cost, because there's toilet removal and reset, tight cuts around the vanity and tub, transitions at the door, and often subfloor work. Don't be surprised when the square-foot number looks higher than the rest of the house, that's normal for bathrooms everywhere.
