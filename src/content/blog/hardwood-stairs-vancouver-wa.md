A staircase is the first thing you see in a lot of Vancouver homes, and in many of them it is still carpeted while the floor around it has gone to hardwood or LVP. Replacing that carpet is one of the most common questions we get, and one of the most misunderstood.

Most people expect to pull up the carpet and find hardwood. Most don't. This guide covers what is usually under carpeted stairs, the two ways to put hardwood on a staircase, how stairs tie in with LVP and engineered floors, and the code and safety details that separate a good staircase from one that looks wrong.

## What is under your carpeted stairs

In most Clark County homes built from the 1970s on, especially tract homes in Cascade Park, Fishers Landing, Felida and similar neighborhoods, carpeted stairs were built with **construction-grade treads**: particleboard, OSB or rough pine, with a rounded nose meant to have carpet wrapped over it. They were never meant to be seen.

So, before you plan on refinishing what is there:

- **Look at the edge** of a tread where the carpet ends at a wall or skirt board, or pull back a corner on the bottom step.
- **Check from below** if the underside of the stairs is open in a closet or garage.
- **Look for staple holes, tack strip and glue.** Even real wood under carpet is usually full of them.

If you find particleboard or OSB, the answer is new treads. If you find solid oak in good condition, which does happen in some older or custom homes, refinishing those treads may be possible. Each one has to be sanded by hand, and you'll need to weigh that work against the cost of new treads.

## Option 1: Retrofit treads over the existing stairs

This is how most carpet-to-hardwood conversions are done in finished homes.

1. Carpet, pad, tack strip and staples come off.
2. The overhanging nose on each old tread is cut flush with the riser below it.
3. A **retrofit tread**, solid hardwood with its own nose, is cut to fit each step exactly and glued and fastened down.
4. A **riser** goes on each vertical face, either matching wood or a paint-grade board.
5. Gaps at the walls and skirt boards are scribed tight or covered with a small molding.

The advantage is that the staircase structure, drywall, skirt boards and railing all stay put. The drawback is that every step gets a little taller. That matters for the riser rules covered below.

## Option 2: Full tread replacement

The other approach is to remove the old treads entirely and install full-thickness solid hardwood treads on the stringers. That gives the cleanest result and keeps step heights where they were, but it often means working around or removing skirt boards and balusters, and it can disturb drywall.

Full replacement makes the most sense when the staircase is being opened up anyway, the railing is being replaced, or the old treads are damaged or squeak badly. We'll tell you at the estimate which approach fits your stairs.

## Painted or wood risers?

**Painted white risers with wood treads** are the most popular look in Vancouver right now. The contrast makes each step easy to see, it suits both modern and farmhouse styles, and scuffs touch up with paint.

**Wood risers matching the treads** give a warmer, more traditional look, and they suit Craftsman-style homes and traditional interiors. They show scuffs from toes more than painted risers do.

Either way, the risers should be planned with the skirt boards and trim so the paint lines are clean.

## Matching stairs to the floor around them

Most staircases are not their own project. They join a hallway, an entry or a landing, and the transition at the top and bottom is where a staircase looks right or doesn't.

- **Solid or engineered hardwood floors:** the treads can be matched in species and stain. Prefinished floors usually have a manufacturer-made matching stair nose. Unfinished treads can be stained on site to match. Our guide to [engineered hardwood](/blog/engineered-hardwood-flooring-vancouver-wa/) covers which products have matching trim.
- **LVP floors:** you can run LVP up the stairs with matching stair nosings, glued down rather than floated. Or you can pair the LVP with real wood treads stained to coordinate. Both are common. Our [LVP installation guide](/blog/luxury-vinyl-plank-installation-vancouver-wa/) covers how LVP stairs are done.
- **Laminate floors:** laminate stairs work, but the nosing is a separate profile and it is harder to make a laminate staircase look seamless. Many homeowners choose hardwood treads instead.
- **Carpet upstairs:** where carpet stays on the upper floor, the top step needs a clean carpet-to-wood transition at the landing.

## Step heights and building code

This is the detail most often missed. Washington follows the International Residential Code for stairs. Under it, risers can be no more than 7 3/4 inches tall, treads at least 10 inches deep, and within one flight the tallest and shortest riser cannot differ by more than 3/8 of an inch.

Here is why that matters. When a retrofit tread is added to every step:

- **The middle risers stay the same,** because each step rises by the same amount.
- **The bottom riser gets taller** by the thickness of the new tread, unless the floor at the bottom also rises.
- **The top riser gets shorter,** unless the floor at the top of the stairs rises by the same amount.

So the new floor at the bottom and top of the staircase has to be planned with the stairs. If the hallway at the top is getting new flooring as part of the project, its thickness can help balance the top step. Uneven steps are one of the main causes of trips on stairs, and you notice the difference right away.

## Safety on hardwood stairs

Hardwood stairs are more slippery than carpet, especially in socks, for small kids and for older dogs.

- **Choose a low-sheen finish.** Matte and satin finishes have more grip than gloss and hide scuffs better. Our [sheen guide](/blog/hardwood-floor-sheen-vancouver-wa/) compares them.
- **Consider a runner** (more below).
- **Keep the handrail solid.** Code requires one on at least one side of a flight with four or more risers. It matters more on wood than on carpet.
- **Light the staircase well** so the nose of each step is visible.

## Stair runners: hardwood and carpet together

A carpet runner over hardwood treads gives you the look of wood at the edges with the traction, quiet and softness of carpet down the middle. It is a good fit for homes with kids, older pets or bedrooms upstairs.

Runners are hand-work. The carpet is cut and bound along its edges, then fitted to the staircase. It can be wrapped over each nose (waterfall) or tucked under each nose to follow the profile of the step, which looks tailored and costs more. Wool and wool-blend runners are worth considering, and runner rods are a decorative option on traditional staircases.

## What drives the price of a staircase

Stairs are priced per step, not per square foot, and a staircase often costs more than people expect. Each tread and riser is measured, cut, fitted and fastened individually. What moves the number:

- **Number of steps,** plus landings and any winders (pie-shaped turning steps)
- **Tread material:** species, solid vs. engineered, prefinished vs. stained on site
- **Retrofit vs. full replacement**
- **Open sides,** where the tread end is exposed and needs a return nose
- **Risers:** wood or paint-grade, and painting
- **Removal** of carpet, tack strip and staples
- **Runner,** if you add one
