Vinyl has come a long way from the yellowing sheet goods people remember from a grandparent's kitchen. Today it's one of the most practical floors you can put in a Vancouver, WA home, and it's the category we get asked about most often by homeowners who want something that looks good and doesn't need babying.

Here's an honest look at what vinyl flooring is, where it belongs, and how to compare products without getting lost in marketing terms.

## The three types of vinyl

All vinyl shares the same basic advantage: a plastic-based core that doesn't absorb water. Where the products differ is form and quality.

**Sheet vinyl** comes in wide rolls, so a typical room can be covered with few or no seams. That makes it the most water-resistant installation type available in this category, since there are barely any places for water to get through. It's also the most affordable option. The trade-off is that it reads less realistic underfoot and at close range than the plank and tile products, and damage is harder to repair because you can't just replace one piece.

**Luxury vinyl plank (LVP)** is the category that has taken over. Long planks, wood-look textures, and click-lock installation. The best of it is convincing enough that people ask if it's real hardwood. It's the default recommendation for most of the homes we work in.

**Luxury vinyl tile (LVT)** is the same technology in a square or rectangular format designed to mimic stone, slate, or ceramic. It gives you a tile look without the cold, the grout maintenance, or the hardness underfoot.

If you want to go deeper on plank specifically, our [luxury vinyl plank page](/luxury-vinyl-plank/) covers it, and our [LVT guide](/blog/luxury-vinyl-tile-vancouver-wa/) covers the tile format.

## Why vinyl makes sense in Clark County

Our climate is the main argument. We get long wet stretches, and that moisture comes into homes on boots, paws, umbrellas, and gear. Entryways, mudrooms, and laundry rooms in this area take more water than the same rooms would in a dry climate.

Wood-based floors, including laminate, have a core that can swell if water sits on a seam. Vinyl doesn't have that vulnerability, because there's no wood fiber in the core to absorb anything. For a Pacific Northwest home, that's not a minor spec difference. It's the reason vinyl keeps winning in the rooms that actually get wet.

## The one spec that actually matters: wear layer

If you take one thing away from this, make it this.

The wear layer is the clear protective coating on top of the printed design layer. It's what stands between your floor and every chair leg, dog nail, and grain of grit tracked in from outside. Thicker wear layer means longer life before the floor starts looking worn.

This matters far more than brand, price, or how the sample looks in a showroom. Two planks that look identical can have very different wear layers, and they will not age the same way. A thin wear layer in a busy household with kids and dogs will scuff and dull within a few years. A thick one in the same house will still look good long after.

When you're comparing products, ask for the wear layer thickness. If a salesperson can't tell you, that tells you something.

## Rigid core vs. flexible

Modern LVP and LVT mostly come with a rigid core, which is worth understanding because it solves a real problem.

Older flexible vinyl conformed to whatever was underneath it, so every imperfection in the subfloor showed through. Rigid core products are stiffer and bridge minor irregularities, which makes them more forgiving over an imperfect subfloor and more stable underfoot. They also tend to feel more substantial and quieter to walk on.

That said, rigid core isn't a license to skip subfloor prep. Significant dips and humps still need addressing before installation, and our [installation guide](/blog/what-to-expect-flooring-installation/) walks through what that involves.

## Where vinyl belongs in a home

**Excellent choices:** kitchens, entryways, mudrooms, laundry rooms, bathrooms, basements, and any space where a pet spends serious time. Basements especially, since concrete slabs bring moisture from below that wood floors don't handle well.

**Good choices:** living rooms, hallways, family rooms, and rental properties where durability and easy replacement matter.

**Consider alternatives:** formal spaces where you specifically want real wood, and rooms with heavy direct sun exposure, since intense sunlight over years can fade some vinyl products. If you want genuine hardwood in the dry rooms, our [hardwood flooring guide](/blog/hardwood-flooring-vancouver-wa-species-finishes/) covers species and finishes.

Plenty of homes we work in use a mix, with hardwood in the formal living areas and vinyl through the kitchen, entry, and utility spaces. That's a sensible approach, not a compromise.

## What vinyl doesn't do

Being straight about the limits:

- It can't be refinished. When a vinyl floor wears out, it gets replaced rather than sanded and renewed. Hardwood can be brought back multiple times over its life.
- It's a manufactured product. If what you want is the character and resale story of real wood, vinyl isn't that, however good the print is.
- Cheap vinyl looks cheap. The gap between entry-level and quality product in this category is large and visible.
- Heavy furniture and appliances can dent softer products, and sharp grit is the main scratching culprit, so entry mats do real work.

## Living with it

Vinyl is genuinely low-maintenance, which is much of the appeal. Sweep or vacuum regularly to keep grit off the surface, damp mop with a cleaner suited to vinyl, and skip abrasive scrubbers and steam mops, since steam can work its way into seams. Felt pads under furniture legs and mats at exterior doors handle most of what would otherwise cause wear.
