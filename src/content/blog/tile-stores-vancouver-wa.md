## Four kinds of places sell tile

**Big-box home centers.** Good for common sizes in stock today: basic ceramic, subway tile, some wood-look porcelain. Prices are competitive on the tile itself. The selection thins out quickly beyond that, the staff are generalists, and installation is subcontracted through the store's program.

**Flooring and tile warehouses.** Large showrooms with lots of stock, including popular large-format and wood-look porcelain at low prices, and plenty of setting materials. They are built for people who know what they want or are doing the work themselves. You get a lot of choice and not much guidance on whether a tile suits your subfloor, your shower or your layout.

**Specialty tile showrooms.** Deep selection from designer lines, handmade tile and natural stone, with staff who know tile well. They often sell to contractors and designers, and many do not install. Great for inspiration and unusual products. Expect to find your own installer.

**Flooring stores that sell and install tile.** A local flooring showroom carries tile from the major manufacturers alongside its other flooring, measures the job and installs with its own crews. The selection is narrower than a dedicated tile showroom's but usually broader than a big box's, and one company is responsible from the order to the grout. This is the category we are in, so read the rest of the guide with that in mind.

None of these is the wrong place to shop. What matters is matching the store to how much help you need. If you are setting tile yourself, a warehouse makes sense. If you want a tile installed and warrantied without coordinating two businesses, buy it where it will be installed.

## The ratings that matter more than the pattern

Most tile is chosen on looks. That is fine as long as it also passes three technical checks, which a good tile store should raise before you fall in love with something unsuitable.

**Where it can go.** Every tile is rated for walls, floors, or both. Wall tile, many glass tiles and some glossy ceramics are too soft or too slippery for floors. Check the box or spec sheet, not the display.

**PEI wear rating (glazed tile).** Rated 0 to 5. For most residential floors, PEI 3 or higher; for kitchens, mudrooms and entries that get grit from outside, 4 is safer. Unglazed and through-body porcelain holds up to wear by its construction.

**Slip resistance (DCOF).** For bathrooms, entries and anywhere that gets wet, look for a DCOF of 0.42 or higher. Polished porcelain and polished marble look beautiful and are slippery on a wet floor. In a Vancouver house, where muddy, wet shoes come in for eight months of the year, entry and mudroom tile should be textured or matte.

A fourth consideration is **porcelain vs. ceramic**. For floors and wet areas, porcelain's density and low water absorption make it the default. Ceramic is lighter, cheaper and easier to cut, and it is excellent on walls and backsplashes.

## Buy enough tile, from one lot

Tile is made in batches, and each batch varies slightly in shade and sometimes in size. The box shows a lot or batch number, and a floor laid from two lots can show a visible line where the color shifts.

- **Buy the whole job at once** and check that every box shares the same lot number.
- **Order overage:** about 10 percent for straight lays, 15 to 20 percent for diagonal, herringbone, large-format tile or rooms full of angles and cuts.
- **Keep a box after the job.** Styles get discontinued, and a spare box is the only reliable way to replace a cracked tile in five years.

Stores that also install measure the room themselves and order the overage the layout actually needs. If you measure and order yourself and come up two boxes short, finding the same lot later can be impossible.

## Big tile needs a flat floor

Large-format tile, meaning anything with a side of 15 inches or more, has taken over tile showrooms, and it looks fantastic: fewer grout lines, a calmer floor, a bigger-looking room. It is also far less forgiving of the floor underneath. Industry standards call for a much flatter substrate for large tile than for small, and a floor that was fine for 12-inch tile can cause lippage, meaning edges that stick up, with 24-by-48-inch porcelain.

A tile store that sells large-format tile should ask about your subfloor. One that installs it should check flatness before quoting, include leveling where needed, and use a leveling system during setting. ## Reading a tile quote

Tile quotes vary more than any other flooring quote, because so much of the cost is labor and preparation that stays hidden once the tile is down. A useful quote lists:

- The tile: manufacturer, series, size, finish and color, plus the quantity including overage
- Removal and disposal of the existing floor
- Subfloor preparation: leveling, underlayment or backer board, uncoupling membrane
- Waterproofing for showers and wet areas
- The setting materials: thinset suited to the tile size, and grout type (cement, or stain-resistant urethane or epoxy)
- Layout: straight, offset, diagonal, herringbone, borders
- Trim, edge profiles, transitions and baseboard work
- Sealing, if the tile or grout needs it

If two quotes differ widely, the difference is almost always in preparation and setting materials, not the tile. Check that both include the same scope. For typical ranges, see our [flooring cost guide for Vancouver](/blog/flooring-cost-vancouver-wa/).

## See full pieces, in your light

A 4-inch chip tells you almost nothing about large-format tile or tile with strong veining. Ask for full tiles, or better, several of them laid out together, so you can see how the pattern repeats and how much the shade varies from piece to piece. Tile with a "V3" or "V4" shade variation rating varies from tile to tile by design, and some people love that while others find it busy across a whole floor.

Look at your finalists at home too. Vancouver's gray daylight cools colors down, so a warm beige in a bright showroom can look flat on a January afternoon, and a crisp white can lean blue. Put the tile on the floor, next to the cabinets or the vanity, at more than one time of day.

## A checklist for comparing tile stores

- Do they ask where the tile is going before recommending it?
- Can they tell you the PEI and slip rating for floor tile?
- Will they measure the room and order the overage, from a single lot?
- Do they check subfloor flatness before quoting large-format tile?
- Does the quote itemize prep, waterproofing, setting materials, grout and trim?
- Who installs, and is there a written installation warranty?
- Can you see full-size pieces or take samples home?
