Tile is the oldest flooring material still in common use, and there's a reason it's lasted. Done properly, a tile floor outlives nearly everything else in the house. In a Vancouver, WA home it's the right answer in specific rooms and the wrong answer in others, and knowing which is which saves a lot of money and regret.

## Porcelain, ceramic, and stone

**Porcelain** is made from denser clay fired hotter, which produces a harder, less porous tile. It resists moisture, wear, and chipping better than standard ceramic, and much of it is through-body colored, meaning a chip doesn't reveal a different color underneath. It's the workhorse for floors, and it's what we'd point most homeowners toward for anything that sees real traffic or water.

**Ceramic** is softer and more porous, easier to cut, and generally less expensive. It's perfectly good for walls, backsplashes, and lower-traffic floors, but on a busy floor it will show wear sooner than porcelain.

**Natural stone** covers slate, travertine, marble, granite, and limestone. Every piece is genuinely unique, and no printed product replicates that depth. The trade-offs are real: stone is porous, so it needs sealing and periodic resealing, some varieties stain easily, and softer stones like marble and limestone scratch and etch. It's the most beautiful and the highest-maintenance option in the category.

## Where tile is the right call

**Shower floors and surrounds.** This is not a preference, it's the correct material. Genuinely wet areas need a tile assembly with proper waterproofing behind it. No floating floor belongs in a shower.

**Bathrooms generally**, where water is routine and the floor needs to tolerate it indefinitely.

**Entryways and mudrooms**, which in Clark County take a beating. Wet boots, dripping coats, and tracked-in grit from October through May. Tile doesn't care.

**Laundry rooms**, where an appliance failure is always a possibility and a waterproof floor with a drain-friendly surface is genuinely useful.

**Kitchens**, though with a caveat covered below.

**Radiant heat installations**, since tile conducts heat well and pairs naturally with in-floor heating.

## Where tile is a harder sell

**Kitchens**, honestly, are a split decision. Tile handles kitchen spills and traffic beautifully. But it's hard on your legs and back if you cook and stand for long stretches, and anything dropped on it breaks. Many homeowners who cook seriously end up preferring something with a little give. Our [luxury vinyl tile guide](/blog/luxury-vinyl-tile-vancouver-wa/) covers the softer, warmer alternative that mimics the same look.

**Living areas and bedrooms**, where the cold and hardness work against comfort and there's no moisture argument for it.

**Basements**, where tile can work but the slab has to be assessed carefully. Concrete moves, and rigid tile over a moving substrate cracks.

## The cold problem is real here

Tile conducts heat away from your feet. In a climate with long cool wet seasons, that's the most common complaint we hear about tile floors, and it's most acute in bathrooms where you're barefoot.

There are two good answers. Radiant floor heating underneath solves it completely and turns the biggest drawback into the biggest luxury, and it pairs better with tile than with any other flooring material. Or you accept it in small rooms with bath mats and choose a warmer material elsewhere. ## Grout is the part people underestimate

The tile itself is nearly indestructible. Grout is where tile floors age.

Unsealed grout absorbs stains, and over a few years it discolors into a shade nobody chose. Sealed grout does much better, and resealing periodically keeps it that way. Grout color choice matters more than people expect: a light grout on a floor shows every bit of traffic, while a mid-tone hides life dramatically better. Narrower grout lines mean less grout to maintain, which is part of why large-format tile has become popular.

## What installation actually involves

Tile is the most labor-intensive common flooring to install, and the part that determines success is invisible when it's done.

The subfloor has to be flat and, critically, **stiff**. Tile is rigid and doesn't tolerate movement. A floor that flexes underfoot will crack tiles and crack grout, and no amount of quality tile prevents it. Over a wood subfloor that usually means backer board or an appropriate underlayment, and sometimes structural reinforcement. In wet areas it means a waterproofing membrane.

Then tile is set in mortar and has to cure before it's grouted, and grout has to cure before the floor is sealed and used normally. This sequence can't be compressed. Plan for the room to be unavailable for several days, and be skeptical of anyone promising a one-day tile floor. ## Choosing tile that works underfoot

One spec worth asking about for floors is slip resistance, particularly in bathrooms and entryways where wet feet and wet boots are the norm. A polished stone or high-gloss tile that looks stunning dry can be genuinely hazardous wet. Textured surfaces and smaller tiles with more grout lines both provide more grip.

Size affects the look as much as the practicality. Large-format tile means fewer grout lines and a more expansive, contemporary feel. Smaller tile and mosaics give more grip and suit traditional spaces and shower floors where the surface needs to slope to a drain.
