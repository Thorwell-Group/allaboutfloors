Hard surface flooring has taken over most of the house over the last decade, and for good reasons we've written about at length. But carpet hasn't gone anywhere, and there are rooms where it's still clearly the right answer.

If you're putting carpet in a Vancouver, WA home, here's how to choose well.

## Where carpet still wins

**Bedrooms.** Warm underfoot on a cold morning, quiet, and soft. This is carpet's strongest case and the room where even committed hard-surface households often keep it.

**Stairs.** Safety is the practical argument. Carpeted stairs provide grip and reduce the consequence of a slip, and they dramatically cut the noise of people going up and down. Hardwood stairs look beautiful and are considerably less forgiving.

**Media rooms and basements.** Acoustics. Carpet absorbs sound instead of reflecting it, which is why a home theater on hard surface sounds harsh. In a basement, it also addresses the cold-concrete problem directly.

**Kids' rooms and play areas.** Falling on carpet is different from falling on tile.

**Any room that feels too loud.** A home that's gone entirely to hard surface often sounds echoey, and homeowners frequently don't diagnose why. Carpet, or even large rugs, is the fix.

## Fiber: the decision that drives everything

**Nylon** is the most resilient common carpet fiber. Resilience means the fibers spring back after being compressed rather than staying crushed, which is exactly what determines whether a traffic path through your living room turns into a visible track. It costs more and it earns it in areas that see real use. For stairs and busy rooms, nylon is the sensible specification.

**Polyester** is naturally stain-resistant because the fiber itself doesn't absorb liquids readily, and it's often softer and less expensive than nylon. Its weakness is resilience: it mats down sooner under sustained traffic. In a bedroom or a guest room that sees light use, that's a fine trade. In a hallway, less so.

**Triexta** is a newer fiber that combines good stain resistance with better resilience than polyester, and it's worth asking about.

**Wool** is the premium natural option, with excellent resilience, natural stain and soil resistance, and a look and feel that synthetics approximate but don't match. It costs accordingly and needs more careful cleaning.

## Pile style changes the look and the wear

**Cut pile** has the fiber tips cut open, giving a soft, plush surface. **Plush** and **Saxony** are smooth and formal and show footprints and vacuum marks, which some people love and some find maddening. **Frieze**, with tightly twisted fibers, hides footprints and traffic better than nearly anything else and is the practical choice for busy homes.

**Loop pile** leaves the fiber loops intact. **Berber** is the familiar version, durable and good at hiding soil, with the caveat that a snagged loop can run. If you have cats, loop pile is worth thinking twice about.

**Cut-and-loop** combines both to create pattern and texture, which is very effective at disguising soil and wear.

The most useful practical rule: **texture hides life**. A smooth plush carpet shows every footprint and every vacuum stripe. A frieze or a patterned cut-and-loop hides an enormous amount.

## The pad is not an accessory

This is where homeowners economize and it's the wrong place.

The pad absorbs the impact of every footstep. Without adequate cushion underneath, that impact goes straight into the carpet fibers and crushes them, and crushed fibers are what makes a carpet look old. Quality carpet on a cheap pad wears out meaningfully faster than the same carpet on a good one, which means saving money on pad costs you money on carpet.

The pad also determines how the floor feels and how quiet it is. Two identical carpets on different pads feel like different products. And on stairs, denser pad matters more than thicker pad, because too much thickness on a stair tread is actually unsafe.

If the budget is tight, buy a slightly less expensive carpet on a good pad rather than the reverse.

## Carpet in a wet climate

A practical Pacific Northwest consideration: carpet and standing water are a bad combination, and unlike hard surface, carpet that gets soaked is genuinely difficult to dry properly. Padding holds moisture, and what's underneath doesn't get air.

The implication is straightforward. Keep carpet out of entryways, mudrooms, bathrooms, and laundry rooms, which is where our climate delivers water into the house. Those rooms want [vinyl](/blog/vinyl-flooring-vancouver-wa/) or [tile](/blog/tile-flooring-vancouver-wa/). Our [waterproof flooring guide](/blog/waterproof-flooring-vancouver-wa/) covers that side, and the [Pacific Northwest climate guide](/blog/best-flooring-pacific-northwest-climate/) covers the whole-house picture.

The other implication is that a good entry system, a proper mat and somewhere to leave wet shoes, protects carpet further into the house more than any carpet feature does. Most of what wears carpet out is grit tracked in from outside, and stopping it at the door is the highest-value maintenance there is.

## Making it last

Vacuum regularly, and more often in traffic paths. This is genuinely the single most effective thing, because it removes the abrasive grit that cuts fibers from within. Deal with spills immediately by blotting rather than rubbing, since rubbing works the stain into the fiber structure. Get carpet professionally deep-cleaned periodically, both to remove what vacuuming can't and because many manufacturer warranties require it. Move furniture slightly now and then so permanent compression doesn't set in, and use pads under heavy pieces.
