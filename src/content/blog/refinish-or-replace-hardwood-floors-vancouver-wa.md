Hardwood is the only floor that gets a second life. Before you spend money replacing yours, it's worth twenty minutes finding out whether you need to.

We get called for both jobs, so we don't have a horse in this race. Here's how we tell the difference.

## The test you can do yourself right now

Take the cover off a floor vent, or find an exposed edge at a doorway or closet threshold. You're looking at the cut end of a plank, which shows you its cross-section.

Find the tongue, the protruding lip on the side of the board. Now measure from the top surface of the plank down to the top of that tongue. That's your remaining sanding depth.

- **About 1/8 inch or more:** you have refinishing life left. A full sand removes roughly 1/32 of an inch.
- **Noticeably less than 1/8 inch:** the floor is near the end. A light screen and recoat may still work, but a full sand risks exposing the tongue and the nails.
- **You can see nail heads or the tongue at the surface:** that floor has been sanded to the limit. Replacement is the honest answer.

Solid 3/4-inch hardwood gets four to seven refinishes over its life. Most Vancouver homes we walk into have used one or two.

## The four things that force a replacement

Sanding depth is the usual answer, but four conditions override it:

**1. Water damage that reached the subfloor.** Surface cupping from a spill often sands flat. A leak that soaked the subfloor doesn't, because the problem isn't the wood you can see. If boards are black, spongy, or lifting, that section is coming out regardless.

**2. Structural movement.** Widespread gaps, boards that rock underfoot, or a floor that's separating from the subfloor across a whole room points at the installation or the joists, not the finish.

**3. Pet urine that soaked through.** This is the one that surprises people. Urine penetrates past the finish into the wood fiber and, in bad cases, into the subfloor. Sanding removes the stain from the top and does nothing about the odor underneath. Affected boards get replaced.

**4. An engineered floor with a thin wear layer.** If the top veneer is around 1mm, there's nothing to sand. This is common on the budget engineered products that went into a lot of homes in the 2000s. Our [engineered vs. solid hardwood guide](/blog/engineered-vs-solid-hardwood-vancouver/) explains how to tell what you have.

## The stuff that looks fatal and isn't

People tear out floors that were perfectly savable. The following all sand out:

- Surface scratches, including dog nail damage
- A worn traffic lane through the middle of the room
- Sun fading near windows and dark rectangles where rugs sat
- Cloudy, peeling, or flaking finish, that's the coating failing, not the wood
- Most surface stains and dents
- A color you hate

That last one matters. A large share of Vancouver refinishing jobs are cosmetic, the floor is structurally fine and the homeowner is done with 2004 orange-toned oak or a glossy finish that shows everything. Sanding to bare wood lets you re-stain to anything, and it lets you change the [sheen](/blog/hardwood-floor-sheen-vancouver-wa/) at the same time.

## How the money actually compares

Refinishing wins on cost when the wood is sound, and it isn't close. You're paying for labor and finish rather than labor, new material, demolition, and disposal.

Replacement makes sense when the disqualifiers above apply, when you want a different species or a wider plank than what's down, or when you're changing material entirely, some homeowners with an aging hardwood floor in a busy household would genuinely be happier on [luxury vinyl plank](/luxury-vinyl-plank/). ## The partial answer nobody offers

There's a middle path that gets overlooked: refinish the floors that are fine, replace only the room that isn't.

If the kitchen took a dishwasher leak but the living room and hallway are healthy, we can replace the damaged section, weave new boards into the existing field, and sand the whole floor as one surface. Done well, the repair disappears into the refinish. It costs meaningfully less than doing the whole house and it keeps the original wood everywhere it's still good.
