A hardwood staircase wears differently from the floor it connects to. The floor gets traffic spread across a whole room. The stairs get every trip up and down concentrated on the same few inches of each tread, right at the nose and down the center. That is why the stairs are often the first place a hardwood finish wears through to gray wood, even when the floor at the bottom still looks fine.

Refinishing stairs is also a different job from refinishing a floor. The big sanders that do a room in an afternoon do not fit on a step, so a staircase is sanded almost entirely by hand. And unlike a bedroom, you cannot just close the door on it for three days.

This guide covers how to tell whether your stairs need refinishing, the three options (recoat, refinish or new treads), how the work is done, matching stain and finish to the floor, and how to keep getting upstairs while the finish dries. It is written for homes in [Vancouver](/stair-flooring-in-vancouver-wa/) and across Clark County, including the many two-story homes in east Vancouver around our showroom.

## Signs your stairs need refinishing

Look at the stairs in daylight, from the bottom and from the side. The most common signs:

- **Gray or bare wood on the nose of each tread.** The rounded front edge takes the most wear. Once the finish is gone there, the wood starts to darken and absorb dirt.
- **A worn path down the middle.** A dull, lighter or grayer stripe where everyone walks, with the original sheen still at the edges.
- **Scratches from pet nails and shoes.** Dogs in particular dig in on the way up. Scratches only in the finish look white or cloudy. Scratches into the wood are darker.
- **Peeling or flaking finish,** often where a cleaner with wax or oil was used and the finish stopped bonding.
- **An orange or amber cast** from an old oil-based finish that has yellowed over the years, especially next to a newer, lighter floor.
- **Treads that no longer match the floor** after the floor was refinished, stained or replaced.

If the finish is dull but intact, with no gray wood showing, you may only need a recoat. If the wood itself is worn, stained or scratched, you need a full refinish.

## Recoat, refinish or new treads

There are three ways to bring a staircase back, and which one you need depends on what is wrong and what the stairs are made of.

| | Recoat | Full refinish | New treads |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | Lightly abrade the existing finish, add a fresh coat | Sand to bare wood, stain if wanted, new finish | Replace the treads (and risers if needed) |
| Fixes | Dullness, light scuffs in the finish | Wear to bare wood, scratches, stains in the wood, color change | Particleboard or OSB treads, cracks, splits, worn-thin treads, heavy paint |
| Can change color | No | Yes | Yes |
| Best when | Finish is intact, no gray wood | Solid hardwood in sound condition | The wood cannot be saved or was never finish-grade |

A recoat only works if the existing finish is clean and still bonded. If someone has used a polish or wax product on the stairs, a fresh coat may not stick, and the stairs need a full sand.

New treads are covered in detail in our [hardwood stairs guide](/blog/hardwood-stairs-vancouver-wa/), including retrofit treads over existing stairs and how step heights change. The rest of this guide is about refinishing what you already have.

## Can your stairs be refinished?

Not every staircase that looks like wood can be sanded.

- **Solid hardwood treads** (oak is the most common in Clark County homes) can be refinished several times over their life.
- **Engineered treads** have a real wood top layer over plywood. They can be refinished if that layer is thick enough, which we check at the edge or from underneath.
- **Prefinished stair nosing** that came with a prefinished floor is often solid wood and can be sanded with the rest, but some products have a thin veneer. We check before we sand.
- **LVP and laminate stairs** cannot be sanded. Worn LVP or laminate treads are replaced, not refinished.
- **Carpeted stairs** in most homes built from the 1970s on have construction-grade treads underneath. They are not finish-grade wood. Pulling the carpet and refinishing is rarely an option there.
- **Painted treads** can be stripped and refinished if the wood underneath is good, but stripping paint from every tread, nose and corner is slow work. Sometimes new treads are the better value.

The free [in-home estimate](/free-estimate/) is where this gets settled. We look at the edge of a tread, under the stairs if it is open, and at any damage, and tell you whether refinishing will give you the result you want.

## How stairs are refinished

A staircase is mostly hand work. Here is how it goes.

1. **Protect the house.** Walls, railings, the floor at the top and bottom, and anything near the stairs are covered. Rooms are closed off to keep dust in.
2. **Remove what is in the way.** Old carpet tack strip, staples, runner rods and loose nails come out, and any holes are noted for filling.
3. **Sand the treads.** Each tread is sanded with smaller hand-held and edge sanders, working through the grits the same way a floor is done.
4. **Hand-sand and scrape the noses and corners.** The rounded nose, the inside corners against the riser, and the edges along the skirt boards and around balusters cannot be reached by a machine. They are hand-scraped and hand-sanded so the color comes out even all the way to the edges.
5. **Fill and repair.** Nail and staple holes, small cracks and gaps are filled with filler matched to the wood or the stain. A cracked or badly damaged tread can be replaced on its own and finished with the rest.
6. **Stain (optional) and test.** If you are changing color, the stain is tested on a sanded tread first, so you choose from the actual wood, not a brochure.
7. **Finish coats.** Several coats of finish go on, with drying time and a light buff between coats so each one bonds to the last.

Modern dust-containment sanders capture most of the dust from the machine work. Hand sanding on stairs still makes some, so we close off the area and clean up as we go.

## Matching stairs to the floor

This is the question we hear most. The answer depends on whether the floor is being done too.

- **Floor and stairs together.** The best result. Everything is sanded at the same time, stained from the same batch and finished with the same product and sheen, so the landing, treads and floor read as one surface. It also costs less per step, because the crew and the setup are already there.
- **Stairs only, site-finished floor.** We sand the stairs and test stain on a tread to match the floor. Treads are often a different board, grade or species than the floor, and new finish looks fresher than a floor that is a few years old, so expect close, not identical.
- **Stairs only, prefinished floor.** Prefinished floors have a factory color and finish that cannot be reproduced exactly on site. We get as close as the wood allows, or suggest a contrast that looks intentional rather than a near miss.

Moving away from orange-toned oak is one of the most common reasons people refinish. Our guides to [hardwood floor staining](/blog/hardwood-floor-staining-vancouver-wa/) and [warm stain colors](/blog/hardwood-floor-stain-colors-warm-tones-vancouver-wa/) cover what each color does on oak.

## Painted risers or stained risers

Refinishing is a good time to change the risers.

**Painted white risers with stained treads** are the most popular look in Vancouver right now. The contrast makes each step easy to see, scuffs from toes touch up with paint, and it brightens a dark stairwell. Many risers in older stairs are paint-grade wood anyway.

**Stained risers matching the treads** give a warmer, more traditional look that suits Craftsman and traditional homes. They show toe scuffs more than paint does.

If you switch to painted risers, plan the paint lines with the skirt boards and trim so the stairs look finished. Our [baseboards and trim](/baseboards-trim/) page covers how trim is handled with a flooring job.

## Choosing a finish for stairs

**Water-based polyurethane** is the usual choice for stairs. It dries fast enough for more than one coat in a day, has low odor in a stairwell that is usually open to the whole house, and stays clear, so it keeps a natural or light stain looking the way you chose it.

**Oil-based polyurethane** gives a warm amber tone that deepens over time. It can suit a traditional staircase or match an older oil-finished floor, but it has a strong odor and much longer drying times, which matters a lot when the stairs are your only way upstairs.

**Pick a low sheen.** Hardwood stairs are slippery in socks, and gloss is the most slippery. Matte or satin gives better grip and hides scratches and dust on the treads far better. Our [hardwood floor sheen guide](/blog/hardwood-floor-sheen-vancouver-wa/) compares them.

## How you get upstairs during the job

In most two-story homes the staircase is the only way to the bedrooms, so this has to be planned before the work starts. The usual options:

- **Every other step.** Half the treads are finished first, so there is always a dry step to use. Then the other half is done. It takes longer but keeps the stairs usable.
- **Half the width at a time.** On a wide staircase, one side is finished while you walk on the other.
- **Timed coats.** Coats go on early so the stairs are walkable in socks by evening, especially with a fast-drying water-based finish.
- **Stay elsewhere for the last coat.** On a big combined floor-and-stairs job, some families plan a night or two away for the final coat and avoid the whole question.

As a guide, you can usually walk carefully in socks about 24 hours after the last coat. Keep pets off for 48 to 72 hours, and wait about a week before putting a runner or stair treads back down while the finish fully cures.

## Pets, kids and keeping the stairs looking new

- **Add a runner** if you have dogs, small kids or older family members. A runner over refinished treads gives grip and quiet down the middle and shows off the wood at the edges. Our [hardwood stairs guide](/blog/hardwood-stairs-vancouver-wa/) covers runner styles.
- **Keep nails trimmed.** Pet nails do most of the scratching on stairs. Our [pet-friendly flooring guide](/blog/pet-friendly-scratch-resistant-flooring-vancouver-wa/) has more on living with dogs on hardwood.
- **Clean gently.** A dry microfiber mop or vacuum with a hard-floor head, and a cleaner made for polyurethane finishes. No wax, oil soap or steam, which can keep a future recoat from bonding. See [hardwood floor cleaning and maintenance](/blog/hardwood-floor-cleaning-maintenance-vancouver-wa/).
- **Recoat before it wears through.** If you recoat when the noses first start to look dull, you may not need a full sand again for many years.

## What drives the price of stair refinishing

Stairs are priced per step rather than per square foot, because nearly every inch of a step is sanded by hand. What moves the number:

- **Number of steps,** plus landings and winders (the pie-shaped turning steps)
- **Open sides,** where the end of each tread is exposed and has to be finished too
- **Risers:** stained to match, or painted, and how many
- **A color change,** which adds stain testing and a stain coat
- **Paint, glue, wax or carpet damage** that has to come off before sanding
- **Repairs,** such as filling, or replacing a cracked tread
- **Whether the floor is done at the same time,** which lowers the per-step cost
