North Clark County has a lot of buildings that are half workplace, half customer space. A contractor's shop with a front office. A light industrial unit with a showroom and a break room. A retail store with a stockroom out back. The concrete in the shop or warehouse bay usually stays as it is. The parts people sit in, walk through and judge you by need a finished floor.

This guide is for owners and managers of those buildings in [Battle Ground](/flooring-in-battle-ground-wa/), [Brush Prairie](/flooring-in-brush-prairie-wa/), [Ridgefield](/flooring-in-ridgefield-wa/) and [La Center](/flooring-in-la-center-wa/). It covers what goes where and how to get it installed without shutting down.

## Split the building by how each area is used

Very few commercial buildings should have one floor throughout. The right approach is to match the floor to each zone.

**The front office and reception.** This is where customers form an opinion. Commercial-grade luxury vinyl tile (LVT) gives you a wood or stone look that holds up to grit and rolling chairs. Carpet tile is the other strong option, quieter in an open office and easy to repair.

**Private offices and conference rooms.** Carpet tile keeps them quiet. Where looks carry more weight, hardwood or a premium plank works in a conference room that does not see shop traffic.

**Showrooms.** A showroom floor has to look good and take steady foot traffic, display moves and the occasional pallet jack at the edge. LVT and porcelain tile are the usual choices. Pick a neutral that does not fight the product you are showing.

**Break rooms and restrooms.** Porcelain tile or a waterproof vinyl. Both handle spills, wet boots and daily mopping. In restrooms, fewer seams make cleaning easier.

**Fitness areas and workstations where people stand.** Rubber flooring cushions feet, cuts noise and takes impact.

## Plan for what comes in from the shop

The biggest difference between a shop or warehouse office and a downtown office is what walks in on people's boots. Gravel, metal shavings, sawdust, mud, oil and water all travel from the bay into the office. In a wet Clark County winter, that is a lot of grit.

Three things help:

- **Walk-off zones.** A commercial walk-off mat or a strip of hard-wearing carpet tile at the door between the shop and the office catches most of it.
- **A hard surface at the transition.** LVT or porcelain right inside the shop door, carpet tile further in where people sit.
- **A color that hides dirt.** Mid-tone, textured floors look clean longer than very light or very dark ones.

## Carpet tile or LVT for the office

Both are good choices, and they solve different problems.

**Carpet tile** comes in squares laid in a pattern. It absorbs sound, which matters in an open office next to a noisy shop. When one square gets stained or worn, you pull it and drop in a spare. Keep a box of extras from the same run.

**LVT** is a hard, water-resistant surface that cleans with a mop. It handles rolling chairs without chair mats wearing through and shrugs off tracked-in grit better than carpet. It is louder underfoot, which you can soften with an underlayment.

A common answer for north county shops: LVT in the reception, hallway and break room, carpet tile in the private offices.

## Scheduling around your business

Most businesses cannot close for a week to get new floors. We schedule commercial installations for nights, weekends or in phased sections, so one part of the building is closed while the rest keeps working. Tell us at the estimate when your slow days are, where your customers come in, and which rooms cannot go offline at the same time.

Furniture, files and equipment have to move for the floor to go down. Plan who moves what, especially computers and anything wired into the wall.

## Older buildings and the subfloor

Plenty of commercial buildings around Battle Ground have been through several tenants. Under the current floor you might find old carpet glue, cracked tile, or patches from a wall that moved. Most commercial floors need a flat, clean surface to bond to or float over. The quote should say what prep is included, so you are not surprised when the old floor comes up.

## What a commercial quote should show

Ask for a written, itemized quote that lists:

- The product for each area and the square footage.
- Removal and disposal of the existing floor.
- Subfloor prep and leveling.
- Transitions between materials and at doorways.
- Base or cove trim.
- The schedule, including after-hours or phased work.

That lets you compare quotes on the same scope instead of a per-square-foot number that leaves things out.
